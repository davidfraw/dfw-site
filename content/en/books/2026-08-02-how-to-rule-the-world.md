*Penguin Press, May 2026\. Read August 2026\.*

Theo Baker won a George Polk Award at eighteen for reporting that ended the tenure of Stanford's president. That fact is the engine of this book and also its problem: a reporting triumph has been asked to carry a memoir, and the weight doesn't distribute evenly.

Reviewers keep calling this two books braided together — the Marc Tessier-Lavigne fraud investigation and the ethnography of Stanford as a machine for manufacturing the next ruling class. There is a third, and nobody counts it: Theo's freshman year in the ordinary sense. The drinking, the girlfriend, the grandfather's death, losing his virginity. That last one is where the seams show. It doesn't earn its place in a book about institutional fraud, and its inclusion reads less like candour than like a memoirist reaching for texture he doesn't need. The three strands don't interweave so much as take turns, and by the end the alternation has stopped generating momentum and started costing it.

He can't stop being his parents' son, and it would be unfair to ask him to. The problem isn't the privilege; it's the timing and the register. The acknowledgment arrives late in the book and in a tone of *everyone knows who my parents are* — true inside a certain world and simply false outside it. Placed earlier and stated plainly, it would have cost him nothing. Placed where it is, it leaves the reader wondering what else is being treated as ambient knowledge.

The reporting on Stanford's entanglement with venture capital is a wild ride, full of details that are genuinely hard to picture from the outside — seminars convened to identify "high-agency individuals," investors shopping among freshmen. But the book treats the capital-to-academy pipeline as self-evidently corrupt, and it does not have to be. What the book would need to show, and never does, is that this is a distinctively academic corruption rather than capital doing what capital does everywhere else. Universities have always been funded by somebody, and proximity between research and money is also how research gets built. The interesting question is where a funding mechanism turns into a selection mechanism, where an institution stops asking who is good and starts asking who is investable, and where random ideas get randomly showered with money. Baker assembles some evidence and a great many anecdotes, and then just leaves it there.

He is unsparing about himself: alcohol, oxycontin, real loss of control. That honesty is the book's best credential. It sits strangely beside how strictly he judges everyone else — classmates convicted on the evidence of one or two odd conversations and filed away as types. He extends himself the full context of a life and grants the people around him a paragraph, or a one-sentence punch.

So what does the book actually manage to establish? That certain ideologies circulate on campus. That the place is administratively no fun. That Stanford will protect its image over nearly anything. All true, and nowhere near enough. We are told that effective altruism and rationalism are in the water without being shown what they claim or why anyone believes them. We get no account of why Stanford in particular keeps producing the Holmeses and the Bankman-Frieds rather than merely producing rich people. And there is no sense of what a repair would look like, or whether Baker thinks one is possible. Diagnosis without mechanism, and without prognosis.

The same thing happens to the book as a memoir. We get a short sample of a year in a life that is still very young and manifestly unfinished, and it lacks texture and contrast because it has not had time to acquire any. University life can be hard; being one of the most-watched journalists in the country at eighteen must be much harder. The book has few answers to either.

There is no clean line to quote, partly because the book keeps reaching for one and the reaching is visible. What stays is Theo collapsing after the oxy. It works because it is the one passage where all three books occupy the same room: the kid, the reporter and the institution at once. It is the only sustained admission that he is not standing outside the system he describes. 

The Tessier-Lavigne chapters are the real thing — patient, corroborated, consequential, and enough to justify the book on their own. Around them sits a portrait of elite formation that is vivid, badly under-argued, and diluted by an unfinished coming-of-age story that belongs in a different book. Read it for the reporting and for the vertigo of the details. Don't expect to come out understanding the system it describes, or the ideas that run it.
